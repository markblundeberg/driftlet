The Frumkin effect: a reaction its double layer repels

Peroxodisulfate (S₂O₈²⁻) reduced to sulfate at a mercury electrode in sodium fluoride, stirred as on a rotating disk: the steady current against the electrode's potential. Negative of the potential of zero charge (pzc) the electrode's charge turns negative and its double layer pushes the anion away from where it reacts, so in dilute salt the current falls as the drive grows. Vary the salt, the potential and the ions' size. (More about the model below.)

About this simulation

Kryukova found this inhibition in 1949 at a dropping mercury electrode, and Frumkin and Florianovich explained it two years later. The electron jumps to the reactant at the plane of closest approach, a few ångströms from the metal, so only the drop across that inner (Stern) layer drives it. The diffuse layer outside sets how much reactant is at the plane: Boltzmann's c e−zFψ/RT, with z = −2 and ψ the diffuse layer's drop. Past the pzc, ψ turns negative, and the plane loses its reactant faster than the inner layer's drop speeds the reaction up. More salt thins the diffuse layer and shrinks ψ, so the fall starts later and goes less deep. (Positive of the pzc it works the other way: the layer draws the anion in, and the wave sits at more positive potentials than the bare kinetics would put it.)

The dashed line is that classic correction, which takes the reactant at the plane to be in equilibrium with the solution just outside the double layer. The full line adds what that leaves out: the reactant has to cross the diffuse layer against its field to reach the plane, and the layer resists it, by a length K = ∫(c/c(x) − 1) dx that grows as the plane empties. That costs little until the reaction at the plane is fast, its rate constant kf (in the readout) past about 1 m/s, so here it trims the far end of the fall, by up to a third at −1.2 V. Most of K builds up within an ångström or two of the plane, closer than the ion's own size, where a continuum picture is at its edge. The simulation (points) follows the full line, to 0.1% where the current is down: that checks the solver against this model, not the model against mercury.

On real mercury the current passes a minimum and rises again at more negative potentials. Here that turns on the ions' size: with small sites (0.1 nm is all but the plain Gouy–Chapman layer of the textbook correction) the current turns and rises within the window, while on sites 0.7 nm across the crowded layer holds ψ more negative and it keeps falling. Real mercury has more in it than this model: molecular models of the reaction (Nazmutdinov, Glukhov, Tsirlina and Petrii, 2002) put the rise down to a reaction layer that thickens, with ions farther out in the diffuse layer taking electrons too, and the rate depends on the cation, which can pair with the anion. Here the reactant reacts only at the plane, and the ions are point charges on sites. A 2023 study, Double-layer inhibition of peroxydisulfate reduction at mercury ultramicroelectrodes, models the transport through the double layer and the electron transfer at a distance together.

The double layer is thousands of times thinner than the stagnant solution (0.5 to 10 nm against 20 µm), so driftlet holds it below the grid: a Gouy–Chapman layer beside the face (diffuse: { epsr }), its ions crowding on sites of the chosen size (Bikerman), in series with an inner layer of 17 µF/cm² (constant here; mercury's is near that at negative charge). The pzc is mercury's in NaF, −0.19 V vs SHE, and the reactant is a trace, 10 µM. The kinetics are illustrative, chosen so the rate constant at the plane stays below 25 m/s in this window (a collision-limited one, √(RT/2πM), is 45 m/s for S₂O₈²⁻): a transfer coefficient of 0.25 for the current as a whole, a Tafel slope of 236 mV per decade (driftlet's α = 0.125, for the two-electron step as written). A Butler–Volmer rate allowed to grow without limit over 3 V would change both lines' shape past the minimum. The theory lines are worked out apart from the solver: the Frumkin correction with the same layer, the layer's resistance by quadrature, and diffusion across the stagnant layer. They leave out migration there (the electrode's field in the solution holds the anion back too), which lowers the plateau by about 1% in the most dilute salt.

The level diagram has the mercury's Fermi level, the S₂O₈²⁻/SO₄²⁻ redox level and its standard level, E° = 1.94 V from the μ° used here, and φ, which is the SHE's level here. The gap of over 2 V between the Fermi level and the redox level is set partly by the oxidant's strength and partly by the trace of sulfate (20 nM) in the bulk. The inner layer's drop and ψ sit between the mercury and the solution's first node, far too thin to see at this scale; the last two panels open the diffuse part up, from the plane outward: φ across it, and each ion's concentration there (log scale). The reactant's thin line is where Boltzmann would put it; the thick one is where it is, drawn down by what the reaction takes.
