Corrosion: a mixed potential and the Evans diagram

Iron in aerated 0.5 M NaCl, behind a layer of still water: left to itself it settles at the corrosion potential, where its own dissolution (Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻) and the reduction of dissolved oxygen (O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻) pass equal currents, and it corrodes at that current. The Evans diagram, each reaction's current against potential, shows where; driving the iron away from it gives the polarization curve you'd measure. Vary how still the water is, and the potential. (More about the model below.)

The Evans diagram: each reaction's current, and the net current you'd measure

About this simulation

Two reactions share one piece of metal, so they share its Fermi level, and with no current leaving the metal, the electrons one makes the other must take. The iron settles where they balance, the corrosion (or mixed) potential Ecorr (Wagner and Traud, 1938), between the iron's own redox level and oxygen's, in equilibrium with neither: iron goes on dissolving at the corrosion current icorr, and oxygen goes on being reduced at the same rate. On the Evans diagram (E against log|i|, in the form Wagner and Traud's theory gives it) that's where the iron's anodic line crosses oxygen's cathodic curve. Oxygen's curve comes down its Tafel line and bends over to a plateau, its limiting current 4FDc/L, set by how fast it diffuses in through the still layer. Where the crossing falls on that plateau, as it does for a layer of half a millimetre, the corrosion rate is oxygen's supply, and stirring the water (a thinner layer) speeds corrosion as 1/L; for the thinnest layers here the crossing moves up the bend, and the kinetics share the control.

Driven below Ecorr, the iron's dissolution dies away while oxygen's plateau carries on: that is cathodic protection, and 0.1 V below Ecorr the iron already dissolves at about 1% of its free rate, for a protection current of about oxygen's limit. Driven above, the iron's current grows tenfold every 60 mV (its Tafel slope, 2.3RT/(2(1 − α)F) with α = ½). That is also how icorr is measured: the anodic Tafel line extrapolated back to Ecorr gives it within a few percent here (the cathodic branch, a plateau, has no line to extrapolate), and so does the polarization resistance, Rp = dE/di at Ecorr, through Stern and Geary's icorr = B/Rp (1957), with B the anodic Tafel slope over 2.3 alone, the cathode being diffusion-limited.

The lines are closed forms, worked out apart from the solver: Butler–Volmer for each reaction at the iron's face, with the Fe²⁺ the iron makes held back by diffusion through the layer (its back reaction sees the surface's Fe²⁺, not the bulk's), and oxygen's supply through the layer in series with its kinetics. The points are the simulation's net current, and the readout gives both corrosion potentials and currents: they agree to 0.1 mV and five digits, and the cathodic side agrees everywhere. Well above Ecorr the simulation runs below the closed form (9% by 0.1 V up), all of it the solution's potential at the face, 2.4 mV there: about half ohmic drop through the layer, and half a diffusion potential from the FeCl₂ built up at the iron diffusing out (Fe²⁺ is slower than Cl⁻). With that taken out the two agree to 10⁻⁴. The simulation is the more realistic curve here: it has what a reference electrode in the bulk would see.

What the model leaves out matters. The profiles below are its, and in a real cell they couldn't last: with Fe²⁺ and OH⁻ together at the face, ferrous hydroxide would be some 10⁵ times past its solubility, and dissolved oxygen oxidises Fe²⁺ in seconds at the pH there. So in reality rust forms within the layer, and the oxygen it takes there never reaches the metal; at most that brings the iron's rate down to two thirds of oxygen's supply. Below about −0.7 V vs SHE, on real steel, water's own reduction to hydrogen joins oxygen's, which is where the window stops. Also left out: films that slow the iron (passivity), chloride's pitting, and an iron surface that isn't one uniform electrode. Its rate constants are illustrative, chosen to put Ecorr in the range measured for steel in sea water (−0.35 to −0.45 V vs SHE), here at its negative end. Oxygen's reduction is written as one four-electron step with α = 0.125, which gives the 118 mV per decade of a rate-determining first electron. The dissolved oxygen is 0.21 mM, salted out of air-saturated 0.5 M NaCl. The potentials are on an ideal-solution scale, from table μ°: E°(Fe²⁺/Fe) is −0.41 V here, where tables give −0.44 V; and the solution's conductivity is the ideal one, a third above real 0.5 M NaCl's.

The level diagram is the mixed potential itself: the iron's Fermi level, and the two couples' redox levels across the still layer, drawn in volts (electron energy is down the page). Electrons leave the iron's redox level for the Fermi level (dissolution) and go on up to oxygen's (reduction); the Fermi level sits between the two, where the flows match. Below it, the concentrations across the layer: oxygen drawn down toward the iron, Fe²⁺ and OH⁻ building up at it.
