Corrosion: a mixed potential and the Evans diagram

Iron in aerated 0.5 M NaCl, behind a layer of still water: left to itself it settles at the corrosion potential, where its own dissolution (Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻) and the reduction of dissolved oxygen (O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻) pass equal currents, and it corrodes at that current. The Evans diagram, each reaction's current against potential, shows where; driving the iron away from it gives the polarization curve you'd measure. Vary how still the water is, and the potential. (More about the model below.)

The Evans diagram: each reaction's current, and the net current you'd measure

About this simulation

Two reactions share one piece of metal, so they share its Fermi level, and with no current leaving the metal, the electrons one makes the other must take. The iron settles where they balance, the corrosion (or mixed) potential Ecorr, between the iron's own redox level and oxygen's, equilibrium with neither: iron goes on dissolving at the corrosion current icorr, and oxygen goes on being reduced at the same rate. On the Evans diagram (E against log|i|) that's where the iron's anodic line crosses oxygen's cathodic curve. Oxygen's curve bends over to a plateau, its limiting current 4FDc/L, set by how fast it diffuses in through the still layer: so where the crossing falls on that plateau, the corrosion rate is oxygen's supply, and stirring the water (a thinner layer) speeds the corrosion in proportion. (Far below Ecorr, water's own reduction to hydrogen would take over; it's left out here, so the plateau runs on.)

The lines are closed forms, worked out apart from the solver: Butler–Volmer for each reaction at the iron's face, with the Fe²⁺ the iron makes held back by diffusion through the layer (its back reaction sees the surface's Fe²⁺, not the bulk's), and oxygen's supply through the layer in series with its kinetics. The points are the simulation's net current; the readout gives the two corrosion potentials and currents, which agree to four digits, and the cathodic side agrees everywhere. Above Ecorr the iron's current grows tenfold every 60 mV, and by 0.1 V up the simulation runs about 10% below the closed form: half of that is the ohmic drop through the still layer (the solution at the face 3 mV up), the rest comes with the Fe²⁺ piling up to tens of millimolar at the iron, where the closed form treats it as a dilute trace. The model leaves out what makes real corrosion hard: the Fe²⁺ and OH⁻ meeting as rust, films that slow the iron (passivity), chloride's pitting, and an iron surface that isn't one uniform electrode. Its rate constants are illustrative, chosen to put Ecorr where steel in sea water sits, around −0.45 V vs SHE; the potentials are on an ideal-solution scale, from table μ°.

The level diagram is the mixed potential itself: the iron's Fermi level, and the two couples' redox levels across the still layer, drawn in volts (electron energy is down the page). Electrons leave the iron's redox level for the Fermi level (dissolution) and go on up to oxygen's (reduction); the Fermi level sits between the two, where the flows match. Below it, the concentrations across the layer: oxygen drawn down toward the iron, Fe²⁺ and OH⁻ building up at it.
