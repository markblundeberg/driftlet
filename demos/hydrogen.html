Hydrogen evolution: Tafel slopes and the volcano

Hydrogen from acid at a metal, made in steps through hydrogen adsorbed on the metal's surface: H⁺ + e⁻ → H(ads) (the Volmer step), then either H(ads) + H⁺ + e⁻ → H₂ (Heyrovsky) or 2 H(ads) → H₂ (Tafel). Which step is slow, and how much hydrogen covers the surface, sets the Tafel slope an experiment measures, 120, 40 or 30 mV per decade (though not one-to-one, as the page shows), and how strongly the metal binds hydrogen sets how fast it all goes: the volcano. About this simulation

Each electron the metal gives the acid passes through a hydrogen on its surface. The Volmer step puts it there: its rate goes as the bare sites θ₀ = 1 − θ and the acid going forward, and as the covered sites θ going back. Then the Heyrovsky step takes it off with a second proton and electron, at a rate going as θ, or two adsorbed hydrogens meet (the Tafel step), at a rate going as θ². At steady state the coverage settles where hydrogen arrives as fast as it leaves. Each step here has α = ½ (for the Tafel step, which moves no charge, that's the share of the binding energy its barrier feels), so an electron step's rate grows e-fold for every 2RT/F (51 mV) of overpotential: 118 mV per decade. If the Volmer step is slow and the surface nearly bare, that's what the current shows (and the harder it's driven, the barer the surface, the fast Heyrovsky step taking each hydrogen as it comes). If it's fast, it holds the coverage at its equilibrium with the potential, θ/θ₀ growing tenfold per 59 mV while the coverage is small: then the Heyrovsky step's rate goes as θ times its own exponential, 40 mV per decade, and the Tafel step's as θ², 30 mV per decade. Driven harder, the surface fills (the coverage panel) and the slopes bend: the Heyrovsky step's up through 118, the Tafel step's to a limit it can't pass, its rate on a full surface, 2F kT eΔGH/kT (kT its rate constant). On a strong binder the surface is nearly full already at equilibrium, so that limit is barely above the exchange current, i₀(1 + eΔGH/kT)²: no Tafel region at all (try platinum's −0.09 eV on the Tafel route). "Slow" in the menu means a small rate constant, 10⁴ or 10⁵ times below the other step's; but each step's rate also carries the binding energy, as e±ΔGH/2kT, and at the ends of the range, driven hard, that can hand much of the control to the other step (on the Volmer route at −0.3 eV and −0.4 V, a little more than the Volmer step keeps).

So the slope alone doesn't name the slow step. A slow Volmer step on a metal that binds hydrogen strongly (ΔGH below zero) gives 40 mV per decade too, on a nearly full surface, the mirror image of a slow Heyrovsky step on a weak binder: in these laws, swapping the two steps and the sign of ΔGH swaps covered and bare sites (try the Volmer route at −0.2 eV and the Heyrovsky at +0.2: the same current at every potential, one's coverage the other's bare sites). And 118 can be either step. What tells them apart is the coverage, which experiments measure only indirectly. The local slope also plunges near equilibrium, where the reverse reaction takes over (no experiment quotes a slope there), and rises past 118 at large currents, as the acid's supply through its 10 µm unstirred layer begins to tell: at −0.4 V the surface still has 90% of the bulk's acid, but the rate goes as its 3/2 power (once for the protons and half again for the diffusion potential their depletion sets up), so each further percent drawn down costs a percent and a half of the rate, and the slope there is 139. The top panel is the free energies along the Volmer–Heyrovsky route at the potential E (against the reversible hydrogen electrode): two protons and electrons, then H(ads) with one of each left, at ΔGH + eE, then H₂ at 2eE, the picture computational catalysis uses. Those are standard free energies, H(ads) at half coverage: at E = 0 the two steps are ΔGH and −ΔGH, one uphill unless ΔGH = 0, but uphill there isn't the same as slow. The dashed level is H(ads) at its actual coverage, ΔGH + eE + kT ln(θ/θ₀), and the drops either side of it are what drive each step: nearly all of the drive falls on the slow step, and the fast one sits near equilibrium. (On the Volmer–Tafel route the first drop is still the Volmer step's.)

That is the volcano: bind hydrogen too weakly and putting it on the surface is uphill; too strongly and taking it off is. The exchange current peaks at ΔGH = 0, as Parsons (Trans. Faraday Soc. 54, 1053, 1958) and, independently, Gerischer argued; Trasatti (1972) plotted measured exchange currents against metal–hydrogen bond strengths, and computed binding energies later put the metals on it (Nørskov and colleagues, J. Electrochem. Soc. 152, J23, 2005), platinum near its top (its computed ΔGH is about −0.09 eV). Here, with the same rate constants throughout and α = ½ for every step, the volcano is exactly symmetric for every route: at equilibrium θ/θ₀ = e−ΔGH/kT, and each step's exchange rate goes as √(θθ₀) (the Tafel step's as θθ₀), which doesn't change when covered and bare swap. Its legs fall a decade per 118 meV (per 59 meV for the Tafel route); other models of the volcano give other slopes, and whether the measured volcano is one at all (or oxides on the strongly binding metals) is debated. The exchange current here is that of the whole reaction, 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂: the current's slope at equilibrium is 2F i₀/RT by every route.

The lines are worked out apart from the solver, from the same laws: the Langmuir form of Butler–Volmer each step has in driftlet (each surface species at its coverage, the bare sites a step takes or frees in its prefactor), the coverage that balances them found by bisection, and the acid's supply in series (H⁺ consumed at the face, ClO₄⁻ in Boltzmann equilibrium, so the acid moves as if H⁺ diffused at twice its rate). The simulated points follow the lines within 4 × 10⁻⁶ (the most at 4.6 A/cm², where the acid at the surface is drawn down), and the volcano's points within 10⁻⁷.

What the model leaves out. The rate constants are illustrative, the same for every ΔGH (real metals differ in more than their binding energy), and α is ½ for every step. The coverage is Langmuir: no interaction between adsorbed hydrogens, which on real platinum spreads its adsorption over a range of potentials, and one kind of site. A real metal can run both routes at once, where here each is chosen. The hydrogen made is held at 1 bar at the surface, so none builds up and no bubbles form; on platinum, hydrogen's own transport away can set a 30 mV per decade slope of its own, and the reverse reaction (oxidising hydrogen) is limited by its supply, neither of which this has. The acid is 1 M HClO₄, ideal, and the face has no double layer.
