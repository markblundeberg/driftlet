Electron transfer through a monolayer: Chidsey's experiment

Ferrocene held at the end of a long alkanethiol in a monolayer on gold, in perchloric acid: nothing diffuses, and the chain slows the electron's tunnelling into milliseconds, so after a potential step the ferrocene's charge relaxes at its electron transfer rate alone, and the current decays as a single exponential. Its rate constant, step by step, against the overpotential, is the plot Chidsey made in 1991, with the parameters he fitted: it bends away from Butler–Volmer's straight line toward a ceiling, as Marcus–Hush–Chidsey kinetics say. (One-level Marcus theory, given its own λ, 1.02 eV, follows it within 9% over his range and turns down beyond it: the dotted line.) The top panel is why the band's sum levels off. Vary the step's overpotential η, the temperature and the reorganization energy λ. (More about the model below.)

About this simulation

Before an electron can leave a ferrocene for the gold (or arrive), the solvent and the molecule around it have to rearrange, as if the charge had already moved; λ is what the whole rearrangement would cost with the electron still in place. Marcus's theory puts the barrier at (λ − D)²/4λ for a driving force D (the free energy the transfer releases), so for one electron level the rate grows with D up to D = λ and falls past it (his inverted region). A metal offers a band of levels, filled to its Fermi level, with an empty level at every energy, the activationless one among them. Summed over them, the rate climbs until the levels at which the transfer is activationless are all on offer; driving harder only adds levels deep in the inverted region, which give almost nothing, and the rate levels off. In the top panel (electron energy upward, from the couple's formal level, at the step's start, when half the ferrocene is oxidised), ferrocene's filled states spread over a Gaussian λ below that level and ferrocenium's empty ones λ above, each about √(4λkT) wide (Gerischer's picture of the couple's states). Oxidation takes electrons from the filled states above the Fermi level into the metal's empty ones, the shaded overlap; reduction the reverse. The rate constant the step measures is their sum, k = νρ kT [I(λ, η) + I(λ, −η)], with I the integral over the band in units of kT (Chidsey's form).

Chidsey (Science, 1991) held ferrocene a fixed distance from gold by self-assembling a mixed thiol monolayer, its ferrocene on a 16-methylene chain, and measured its electron transfer in 1 M HClO₄ from 1 to 47 °C over reaction free energies from −1.0 to +0.8 eV, by potential steps, judging the sites' kinetic homogeneity by the single exponential: rate constants from 1 per second to 2 × 10⁴ per second. Marcus–Hush–Chidsey kinetics fitted them with λ = 0.85 eV and a tunnelling prefactor νρ = 6.73 × 10⁴ s⁻¹ eV⁻¹ (7 × 10⁴ in his abstract), this page's values, the prefactor set to give the decay rate constant he measured at the formal potential, 2.5 s⁻¹ (the same monolayer's voltammetry gave a standard rate constant of 1.3 s⁻¹ by Laviron's analysis; Devaraj and colleagues (2006), from his group, later measured 2.2 s⁻¹ with a triazole link in place of the ester). With the integral exact, those parameters give 1.30 s⁻¹ each way at the formal potential at 25 °C, so 2.60 s⁻¹ for the relaxation a step measures, 4% above the 2.5 they were set to (the paper doesn't give enough of its numerics to say why); 0.58 each way at 1 °C and 2.46 at 47 °C, 4.2-fold where he measured 3.7-fold, an activation energy of 0.24 eV, a little above λ/4. Over his range a single level with λ = 1.02 eV draws nearly the same curve (within 9%); the band's sum is the one he fitted, and its λ, 0.85 eV, compares reasonably, as he noted, with the 0.94 eV he calculated for the solvent's reorganization. The chain is what makes the experiment possible: each methylene cuts the tunnelling roughly e-fold (as later measurements put it; the 1991 paper left the distance dependence to later work), which brings these rates within reach of a potential step, ahead of the cell's own charging time. The ceiling is νρ kT √(4πλ/kT) = 3.5 × 10⁴ s⁻¹, half of which the oxidation reaches at η = λ; at 0.8 V the rate is 41% of it, and by 1.5 V it has all but reached it (99.9%). By 0.8 V Butler–Volmer, with the same rate at the formal potential, would be 500 times faster. Driven hard, the rate depends on the temperature far less than at the formal potential, as he found: 10% between 1 and 47 °C at 0.8 V against fourfold, and 2% if the electronic coupling, rather than νρ, is held fixed (νρ in this form carries 1/√T; he held it fixed across his temperatures).

The simulation runs the experiment: the gold at the couple's formal potential, a step to η, and the current through the gold as the ferrocene's coverage moves to its new equilibrium. Its rate constant is the slope of a straight line fitted to ln i over the decay (between half a decay time and three), with nothing of the theory in the fit; the points follow the line, worked out apart from the solver (the integral by Simpson's rule), within 2 × 10⁻⁴. The couple is driftlet's surface species at the face (surface, with the reaction Fc + ClO4- = FcClO4 + e-, Marcus–Hush–Chidsey kinetics), and the coverage is held by the face, not carried by the solution.

What the model leaves out. Surface species here carry no charge, so ferrocenium is written as an ion pair with perchlorate, which the acid's 1 M supplies, as it's often described in these monolayers (ion pairing there is well documented: Rowe and Creager, Langmuir 7, 2307, 1991). The face has no capacitance, so no charging current follows the step, where a real measurement must separate the two (his cell's time constant was 3 µs). All the potential the step applies drives the reaction, which Chidsey calculated is nearly so here: with the ferrocene that far out, the interfacial drop falls almost entirely between it and the gold. Not modelled: the spread of rates a real monolayer can show, or the solution's resistance between the gold and a reference. The gold's density of states and the coupling are taken as flat in energy; λ and νρ are held fixed as the temperature changes (and as the λ slider moves); and the rate is symmetric, one λ for both directions. Later work has refined each of these (kinetic dispersion, asymmetric forms of the theory): the fit here is a landmark early one. The ferrocene covers 2% of the monolayer's sites (1.5 × 10⁻¹¹ mol/cm², an illustrative coverage: k doesn't depend on it).
