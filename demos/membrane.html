A cation-exchange membrane between two salt solutions

A cation-exchange membrane between 0.1 M NaCl and a more dilute solution, at open circuit: the voltage between Ag/AgCl electrodes in the two solutions, against the Teorell–Meyer–Sievers theory, and each ion's level through the membrane. Vary the dilute side, the membrane's fixed charge and the unstirred layers of water beside it. (More about the model below.)

About this simulation

Each solution is stirred, with an unstirred layer of water between it and the membrane. The membrane's fixed negative charge keeps chloride out (Donnan exclusion), so the salt that leaks through is carried almost entirely by Na⁺. Na⁺ then stays nearly level across the membrane, in equilibrium between the solutions, and the whole salt difference lands on Cl⁻, which the electrodes sense: E = 2t₊VT ln(c₁/c₂), with t₊ → 1 for an ideal membrane. (The often-quoted 59 mV per decade "membrane potential" is this minus the electrodes' own Nernst terms, a conventional split.) With less fixed charge, chloride gets in and t₊ falls toward its free-solution value, D₊/(D₊ + D₋) = 0.40.

The dashed curve is the Teorell–Meyer–Sievers theory: a Donnan equilibrium at each face and diffusion between. It's the limit of thin unstirred layers. Thicker ones polarise, since the salt leaking through piles up on the dilute side and is drawn down on the other, so the membrane sees less of the difference.

The level diagram uses the species voltage Vi = μ̄i/(ziF): a species' electrochemical potential per unit charge, in volts, so electrons and ions share one axis. The current each ion carries flows down slopes in its own Vi (cations move downhill, anions uphill), and it's in equilibrium where its Vi is level. Each ion's Vi (thick) is continuous through each face, while its standard level V°i (thin, where Vi would sit at the reference concentration) steps there, following φ. Those steps are the Donnan potentials, bookkeeping that cancels from anything measured. Na⁺ is drawn 4.4 V higher (⌇) to share the plot.
