A redox electrode's impedance: the Randles circuit

Platinum in 1 mM Fe³⁺ and Fe²⁺ with KCl (and 10 mM HCl), at rest, behind a stagnant layer of solution: its impedance spectrum, simulated from the ions and the reaction, against the Randles equivalent circuit with every element worked out from the same physics. A semicircle for the reaction and its double layer, then diffusion's arc: a 45° line while the diffusion is short of the stirred bulk (a thick stagnant layer shows it best), bending back once it reaches it. Vary the rate constant, the salt and the stagnant layer. (More about the model below.)

About this simulation

A small sine wave of voltage on the electrode (small next to RT/F, 26 mV, so the response is linear) drives three things at once. Charge piles onto the double layer, like a capacitor Cdl. Fe³⁺ is reduced and Fe²⁺ oxidised a little faster and slower in turn, which near rest is a resistor, the charge-transfer resistance Rct = RT/(Fi0), with i0 = Fk⁰c the exchange current (k⁰ in m/s; so k⁰ = RT/(F²Rctc) gives it back from a fit). And the reaction's own small changes in concentration have to diffuse to and from the bulk, which lags: the Warburg impedance ZW, at first σ(1 − i)ω−½. The current to all of them crosses the solution, Rs. So Z = Rs + 1/(iωCdl + 1/(Rct + ZW)), Randles' circuit. Its corner at ω = 1/(RctCdl) is the top of the semicircle while the Warburg there is small; then the 45° line takes over; and once the diffusion reaches through the stagnant layer to the stirred bulk (about D/(2πL²) in frequency), ZW stops growing and the spectrum bends back to the real axis at Rs + Rct + RW, with RW = (RTL/F²c)(1/DFe³⁺ + 1/DFe²⁺). That's the transmissive finite Warburg of a Nernst diffusion layer (fitting programs' "Ws"); a rotating disk is the usual way to make one.

driftlet knows nothing of the circuit: it solves the ions, the potential and the reaction for their small oscillation at each frequency. The circuit's elements here come from the same physics, by hand: Rs = L/κ from the solution's ideal conductivity; Cdl, an inner (Stern) layer of 20 µF/cm² in series with the diffuse layer's εκD (Gouy–Chapman at zero charge); Rct from k⁰; and ZW for a layer of thickness L ending at a fixed bulk, (RT/F²c)·Σ tanh(L√(iω/D))/√(iωD) over Fe³⁺ and Fe²⁺. The readout gives how far the two disagree, under 1% across these controls. Most of that shrinks with less iron: the iron's own migration, which the circuit leaves out (in the most dilute salt Fe³⁺ and Fe²⁺ carry a few percent of the current, but both are cations pushed the same way, and their corrections to RW mostly cancel: a couple with one neutral form would be off by more). The rest, a tenth of a percent or two, isn't traced here. (Not the Frumkin effect: at rest a change in ψ moves the forward and back reactions alike, since zFe³⁺ − zFe²⁺ is the electrons' charge, as for any couple, so Rct sees the whole of the overpotential.)

Three choices make this cleaner than a measurement. Rs here is only the stagnant layer's, to a reference at its edge; in a real cell it's set by the cell's shape and where the reference sits, and is often tens of times more. The double layer (driftlet's diffuse: { epsr }, below the grid) is set to have no charge at rest; real platinum at this potential sits well positive of its zero charge, with chloride adsorbed, and off zero charge ψ would shift the exchange current (the Frumkin effect, on its own page) and Cdl. And it's an ideal capacitor, where real solid electrodes give depressed arcs, which fits model with a constant-phase element. The solution has 10 mM HCl so that Fe³⁺ doesn't hydrolyse and precipitate; in reality the chloride also complexes Fe³⁺ and speeds its reaction, which this model leaves out. k⁰ is illustrative, in the range reported for this couple at platinum.
