Graded-base transistor

An npn transistor of AlGaAs and GaAs whose base is an alloy, its aluminium fraction falling toward the collector, so its band gap narrows across it. The holes in the p-type base keep their band edge flat, so the whole narrowing tilts the conduction band: a field that only the electrons feel, pushing them across the base toward the collector. The grading sets how hard (or, reversed, how hard it holds them back), and the base transit time is drawn against Kroemer's formula for it. (More about the model below.)

About this simulation

The transistor is a stack of layers along one line: an n-type Al0.3Ga0.7As emitter (5×10¹⁷ cm⁻³), graded over 30 nm to the base's starting composition; a p-type base 100 nm wide (10¹⁸ cm⁻³), its aluminium fraction going linearly from xE at the emitter to xC at the collector; and an n-type GaAs collector (10¹⁶ cm⁻³, on a 3×10¹⁷ subcollector), at 2 V. The base contact is the one-dimensional idealisation of one: it holds the holes' level at VBE across the base, as a base contact running along it would. Each layer of alloy is driftlet/kit's alloy() between GaAs and Al0.3GaAs (the usual estimates: a gap of 1.424 + 1.247x eV, 62% of its change in the conduction band, an electron mass of 0.067 + 0.083x), each value it changes going linearly with the composition. The grading control is the gap's drop across the base, 1.247(xE − xC) eV: positive narrows it toward the collector, negative widens it.

The band diagram is electron energy up. In the base the holes are the majority: their density is the doping, so with the valence band's density of states the same throughout, Ev stays level with their quasi-Fermi level, flat, and the gap's narrowing all goes into Ec, a slope of up to 1.2 meV/nm. That slope is a quasi-field: it acts on the electrons alone, where an electric field would push the holes too. (How much of the slope to call an electric field is a convention in a graded material, since φ there isn't fixed by the physics; the band edges are.) The electrons the emitter injects drift down it as they diffuse, so they cross faster, and fewer wait in the base.

The transit time is the base's charge over the current it carries, τB = Qn/JC, with Qn the electrons in the neutral base (where the holes are over half the doping). For a quasi-field that drops the electrons' potential by η thermal voltages across a base of width W, Kroemer's formula is τB = (W²/Dn)[1/η − (1 − e−η)/η²], which is W²/2Dn without grading. Here η is the conduction band's drop less the change in its density of states, ΔEc/kT − ln(Nc,E/Nc,C) (the electron mass falls with the aluminium, taking about 0.15 kT off η at the steepest grading). The width is the one fitted number: taken from the ungraded transistor at the same VBE, it comes out 3 nm wider than the neutral base, as the base's edges are spread over about a Debye length (4 nm here), and each graded point uses it scaled with its own neutral width. The simulation follows the formula to within 0.4% across the whole range, slowed down as well as sped up, at every VBE the slider reaches (the test suite holds the page to it).

The current gain β = JC/JB rises with the grading: the base current is mostly the electrons recombining on the way across, Qn/τn with a lifetime τn of 0.5 ns (from half of it, at the most retarding grading and the lowest VBE, to 90% at the most accelerating; the rest recombines in the emitter's junction), and there are fewer of them to recombine. What drift–diffusion leaves out matters in a real base this thin: an electron crosses 80 nm in under a picosecond, only a few times its momentum relaxation time (about 0.08 ps), so part of its trip is ballistic, and it leaves the base at a saturated velocity rather than one set by its mobility. Real HBT bases are also doped well past 10¹⁹ cm⁻³, where the holes are degenerate; 10¹⁸ keeps them within the Boltzmann statistics used here.
