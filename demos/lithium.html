Lithium plating: a Tafel plot that bends

Lithium plated onto, and stripped from, a lithium microelectrode in 1 M LiPF₆ in EC:DEC, with the Marcus–Hush–Chidsey kinetics fitted to measurements of exactly this: the steady current against the overpotential η, simulated from the ions and the reaction, against Butler–Volmer's straight Tafel line with the same exchange current. The top panel is why they part: the metal's electrons, filled to its Fermi level, against the states a lithium ion offers. Vary the reorganization energy λ, the electrode's radius and η. (More about the model below.)

About this simulation

Before an electron can land on a lithium ion, the solvent around the ion has to rearrange, partly, as if the charge had already gone; λ is the energy that rearrangement would cost with the electron still in the metal. Marcus's theory puts the barrier at (λ − ΔG)²/4λ for a driving force ΔG, so the rate for one electron level grows with ΔG up to ΔG = λ and then falls again (his inverted region: the dotted line). A metal offers a whole band of levels, filled up to its Fermi level, and Chidsey (1991) added them up: driven harder, the Fermi level rises past the ion's states, and the deeper electrons, for which the jump is still downhill but not too far, take over. The rate then levels off instead of falling. In the top panel (electron energy upward, from the couple's level), the ion's empty states spread over a Gaussian λ above that level, its filled ones λ below, each about √(4λkT) wide (Gerischer's picture). Plating takes electrons from the metal's filled states into the ion's empty ones, so its rate is the shaded overlap below the Fermi level; stripping the reverse. Near η = 0 it's Butler–Volmer with α = ½; driven harder, the local slope falls, by Marcus's parabola as α ≈ ½ − F|η|/2λ (for a λ much larger than kT; the spread of the metal's electrons slows it, by 30% at 0.22 eV), and once the Fermi level is well past λ the whole Gaussian is below it, and the kinetics can go no faster, whatever the drive: i0√(4πλ)/I(λ, 0) in the notation of driftlet's lambda, 295 mA/cm² here.

Boyle and colleagues measured lithium plating and stripping in four carbonate electrolytes by fast voltammetry at ultramicroelectrodes (ACS Energy Letters, 2020), out to 0.25 V either way, and found the current curving away from Butler–Volmer. Sripad, Korff, DeCaluwe and Viswanathan fitted Marcus–Hush–Chidsey kinetics to their data: in EC:DEC, λ = 0.22 eV and an exchange current of 8.6 mA/cm², this page's defaults. They evaluated the integral with Zeng and colleagues' closed form, which is 4% low at η = 0 and 4% high at 0.25 V for this λ; driftlet computes it exactly, and the same curve then has λ = 0.23 eV and an exchange current 3% higher (within 1% across the measured range). On a 5 µm electrode, by 0.25 V Butler–Volmer with α = ½ would pass three times the current, and past the data the two part for good: Marcus–Hush–Chidsey levels off below its kinetic ceiling (a third of a 5 µm sphere's transport limit; with the salt's supply in series, the plateau is 225 mA/cm²), where Butler–Volmer runs on to that limit. That plateau is what the fitted model predicts beyond what was measured, not a measurement. On a larger electrode transport caps the current first, and the bend is hidden behind it: hence the microelectrodes. A larger λ, at the same exchange current, moves the bend out and the plateau up (try 0.5 eV).

The lines are worked out apart from the solver: the integral by Simpson's rule, and the steady supply of a binary salt to a sphere, exact for this model. PF₆⁻, which the lithium neither takes nor gives, sits in Boltzmann equilibrium in the potential the current sets up, so the salt moves as if Li⁺ diffused at twice its rate, and the Li⁺ at the surface is c − jr(1 − r/R)/(2DLi⁺) for a plating flux j, a radius r and the bulk at R = 100r. That one expression holds the depletion, the ohmic drop and the diffusion potential together; with the kinetics in series it leaves one equation in j. The simulated currents (points) follow the full line within 3 × 10⁻⁵ here, and 3 × 10⁻⁴ anywhere on the sliders. The thin lines are the kinetics alone, with the surface at the bulk's concentration: what a fit to the measured currents is after.

What the model leaves out. It's an ideal solution: activities are concentrations and the conductivity is Nernst–Einstein's from the ions' diffusivities (2.4 S/m), where real 1 M LiPF₆ in carbonates is concentrated enough for ion pairing and conducts well under half as well. The diffusivity and transference number are typical of LiPF₆ in carbonates (salt diffusivity 3 × 10⁻¹⁰ m²/s, t+ = 0.38), and the concentration, 1 M, is the usual one rather than one the fit reports. The electrode is a sphere at steady state, where the measurements were transient voltammetry at a disk; the metal's surface is smooth and fixed (no growth, no dendrites) and bare: the solid-electrolyte interphase every real lithium surface carries isn't modelled. And the rate is symmetric Marcus–Hush–Chidsey, one λ for both directions, as the fit has it. Below it, the salt, depleted toward the electrode while plating and piled up while stripping.
