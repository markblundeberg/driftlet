Lithium plating: a Tafel plot that bends

Lithium plated onto, and stripped from, a lithium microelectrode in 1 M LiPF₆ in EC:DEC: the steady current against the overpotential η, simulated from the ions and the reaction, with Marcus–Hush–Chidsey kinetics as fitted to measurements of exactly this, and Butler–Volmer's straight Tafel line for comparison. Measured, the current bends away from Butler–Volmer; two forms of Marcus's theory fit that bend equally well, and part only past what was measured, where this one levels off. The top panel shows why it does: the metal's electrons, filled to its Fermi level, against the states lithium offers. Vary the reorganization energy λ, the electrode's radius and η. About this simulation

Before an electron can land on a lithium ion, the solvent around the ion has to rearrange, as if the charge had already gone; λ is what the whole rearrangement would cost with the electron still in the metal, and the barrier is the part of it the jump needs. Marcus's theory puts that barrier at (λ − D)²/4λ for a driving force D (the free energy the transfer releases), so the rate for one electron level grows with D up to D = λ and then falls again (his inverted region). A metal offers a whole band of levels, filled up to its Fermi level, and summed over them (the theory of Marcus, Gerischer, and Levich and Dogonadze, which Chidsey's 1991 experiment made the standard test of): driven harder, the Fermi level rises past the ion's states, and the deeper electrons, for which the jump is still downhill but not too far, take over. The rate then levels off instead of falling. In the top panel (electron energy upward, from the couple's level), the empty states Li⁺ offers spread over a Gaussian λ above that level, and the reduced form's, lithium's, filled ones λ below, each about √(4λkT) wide (Gerischer's picture). Plating takes electrons from the metal's filled states into the empty ones, so its rate is in proportion to the shaded overlap below the Fermi level; stripping the reverse. (The page holds the exchange current fixed as λ changes, as a fit to measurements does, so the proportion changes with λ: a larger λ shrinks the overlap, and the prefactor grows to keep the exchange current.) Near η = 0 it's Butler–Volmer with α = ½; driven harder, the local slope falls, by Marcus's parabola as α ≈ ½ − e|η|/2λ (for a λ much larger than kT; the spread of the metal's electrons slows it, by 30% at 0.22 eV), and once the Fermi level is well past λ the whole Gaussian is below it, and the kinetics can go no faster, whatever the drive: i0√(4πλ/kT)/I(λ, 0), with I the integral over the band at the couple's level, roughly 300 mA/cm² here.

Boyle and colleagues (ACS Energy Letters, 2020) measured lithium plating and stripping in ten electrolytes by fast voltammetry, at 10 V/s and faster, on lithium plated onto a tungsten ultramicroelectrode 12.5 µm in radius, out to 0.25 V either way, and found the current curving away from Butler–Volmer. They fitted one-level Marcus–Hush kinetics (in EC:DEC, λ = 0.30 eV). Sripad, Korff, DeCaluwe and Viswanathan refitted four of the carbonate electrolytes both ways: in EC:DEC, Marcus–Hush with λ = 0.34 eV and an exchange current of 8.8 mA/cm² (the dotted line), and Marcus–Hush–Chidsey with λ = 0.22 eV and 8.6 mA/cm² (this page's defaults). Over the measured range the two lie within 8% of each other, and Sripad and colleagues found them indistinguishable there: it takes measurements past 0.25 V to tell them apart, where Marcus–Hush–Chidsey levels off and one-level Marcus–Hush turns down. So the plateau here is the fitted model's prediction, not a measurement, and only roughly known: an uncertainty in λ like the ±0.02 eV Boyle and colleagues give for theirs moves it by a quarter either way. They evaluated the integral with Zeng and colleagues' closed form, 4% low at η = 0 and 4.5% high at 0.25 V for this λ; with the integral exact, as driftlet computes it, the same curve has λ of about 0.23 eV (0.226 to 0.233, by how a fit weighs the curve) and an exchange current 3 to 6% higher, and a ceiling of 335 to 350 mA/cm². The page's defaults are their fitted values, with the integral exact. By 0.25 V Butler–Volmer with α = ½ would pass six times the kinetic current (three times the steady current on a 5 µm sphere, where transport caps it).

Both fits treat plating as an electron's jump to a lithium ion at the surface. Metal deposition is often described instead as the ion's own transfer, coupled to the electron's (Gileadi, 2011; Pinto, Schmickler and colleagues, 2013), and whether lithium's is limited by the electron transfer is debated; Boyle and colleagues argue it is, having measured on fresh lithium within a second, before an interphase forms, and set desolvation aside. This page shows what the fitted model implies, not that the mechanism is settled. (The clean demonstration of the bend toward it is Chidsey's: ferrocene held at the end of a monolayer on gold, nothing diffusing, measured over 1.8 V of driving force.)

The lines are worked out apart from the solver: the integral by Simpson's rule, and the steady supply of a binary salt to a sphere, exact for this model. PF₆⁻, which the lithium neither takes nor gives, sits in Boltzmann equilibrium in the potential the current sets up, so the salt moves as if Li⁺ diffused at twice its rate, and the Li⁺ at the surface is c − jr(1 − r/R)/(2DLi⁺) for a plating flux j, a radius r and the bulk at R = 100r. That one expression holds the depletion, the ohmic drop and the diffusion potential together; with the kinetics in series it leaves one equation in j. The simulated currents (points) follow the full line within 3 × 10⁻⁵ here, and 5 × 10⁻⁴ anywhere on the sliders. The thin lines are the kinetics alone, with the surface at the bulk's concentration: what a fit to measured currents is after.

What the model leaves out. It's at steady state on a sphere, where the measurements were fast scans, whose diffusion layer is thinner than steady state's: on Boyle's 12.5 µm at steady state, transport takes much of the bend (try it), which the scans got past. It's an ideal solution: activities are concentrations and the conductivity is Nernst–Einstein's from the ions' diffusivities (2.4 S/m), where real 1 M LiPF₆ in carbonates is concentrated enough for ion pairing and conducts well under half as well. The diffusivity and transference number are typical of LiPF₆ in carbonates (salt diffusivity 3 × 10⁻¹⁰ m²/s, t+ = 0.38), and the concentration, 1 M, is the usual one rather than one the fits report. The metal's surface is smooth and fixed (no growth, no dendrites) and bare: the solid-electrolyte interphase every lithium surface soon carries isn't modelled. And the rate is symmetric, one λ for both directions, as the fits have it. The last two panels show the electrons' Fermi level and the ions' voltages, Vi = μ̄i/ziF (V° the standard level; electron energy down the page here), and the salt, against x, measured from the inside of a lithium shell half the radius thick (modelled so its level shows): the dotted line, at x = r/2, is the electrode's surface. The step there drives the reaction; the salt is depleted toward the electrode while plating and piles up while stripping.
