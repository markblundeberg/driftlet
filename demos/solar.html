Silicon solar cell under light

A silicon solar cell, a thin n⁺ emitter on a 60 µm p base, under uniform light: its current–voltage curve, with the short-circuit current and open-circuit voltage checked against textbook theory, and the quasi-Fermi levels splitting under the light. Vary the bias, the light and the base's lifetime. (More about the model below.)

About this simulation

Light is a reservoir: the reaction photon → e⁻ + h⁺, with the photons' μ far above the gap, generates pairs uniformly. The band diagram uses the species voltage Vi = μ̄i/(ziF): a species' electrochemical potential per unit charge, in volts, so electrons and ions share one axis. Carriers drift and diffuse down slopes in their own Vi: for electrons and holes these are the quasi-Fermi levels (thick), drawn with their standard levels V°i, the band edges (thin). Under light the quasi-Fermi levels split even at zero bias; at open circuit they split by qVoc and no current flows.

A pair made in the base is collected if its electron reaches the junction before it recombines or reaches the back contact, which is a sink here (there's no back-surface field). Integrating that chance over the base gives Ln tanh(Wb/2Ln) of collecting width: about Ln for a short diffusion length, but never more than half the base however long it is. The readout checks Jsc = qG(that + the depletion layer + the emitter's share) and Voc = VT ln(Jsc/J₀ + 1), with J₀ from the dark curve. The dashed curve is the dark curve shifted by Jsc, the superposition principle.
