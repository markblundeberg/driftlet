Bistability behind a resistor

Peroxodisulfate reduced at mercury in dilute salt: past the potential of zero charge its current falls as the electrode goes more negative (the Frumkin effect). Drive it from a source through a resistor and one source voltage can hold two steady states, a busy one and an idle one, with a third between them that can't last. Sweep the source down and back and the current jumps at different voltages each way. Near the upper jump, the busy state starts to ring before it falls. Each traced state is marked by driftlet's stability(): solid where every small disturbance dies away, dashed where one grows. (More about the model below.)

About this simulation

The electrode is the one on the Frumkin page, with a thousand times the peroxodisulfate (1 mM, in 1 mM NaF), so that its current is large enough for a resistor to matter: up to about 9 A/m² at the mass-transport limit across the 20 µm stagnant layer. Between −0.2 and −0.7 V (against SHE) the double layer pushes the anion away from the plane where it reacts, and the current falls by a factor of eighty. That falling stretch is a negative resistance: more drive, less current.

The middle chart is the classic way to read it. The grey curve is the electrode on its own, held at each potential E (a potentiostat with no resistance in the way: every state on it is stable). The straight line is the resistor's: the current it lets through, (E − U)/R, for the electrode at E. Each crossing is a steady state. Where the line is steep (small R) it crosses once. Where it's shallow enough to lie along the falling stretch, it can cross three times, and the middle crossing is unstable: let the electrode drift a little negative and its current drops, so less flows through R, so less of the source's voltage is lost across it, and the electrode is pushed further negative still. The two outer crossings hold. The sweep leaves the busy state where the line stops crossing the busy side (it lies tangent there: a fold), and comes back only where it stops crossing the idle side, so the curve against U (top chart) has an S in it, and the up and down sweeps part.

The top chart is traced by branch(), which follows the steady state round the S's folds (pseudo-arclength continuation) and asks stability() at each point whether a small disturbance grows. The folds it finds should be the tangent points of the load line on the grey curve; the readout below the charts compares the two. Marked differently along the busy branch, just before its fold: a pair of modes that oscillate as they grow (a Hopf point, where the pair's growth rate crosses zero). The double layer charges through R (RC is 17 ms at 0.1 Ω·m²) faster than the reactant's supply can keep up, and the two together ring. Set U a millivolt or two past that point, in slow motion: the busy state swings with a growing amplitude for a few cycles, and then falls to the idle one. Electrochemists know peroxodisulfate reduction as an oscillator of this kind, with the reactant's slow diffusion as the slow variable. At the smaller resistances the idle branch has a Hopf point of its own, a tenth of a millivolt before its fold and slower (about 9 rad/s at 0.05 Ω·m²): too narrow to see on the chart, but listed in the readout.

Stability depends on what is held. With the source held (here), states on the falling stretch can be stable or not depending on R; with the current held instead (R very large), every state on it is unstable; and under a potentiostat (R → 0) none is. The marks are always for the device as driven.

The model: strictly neutral solution with the double layer below the grid (a Gouy–Chapman layer on a lattice of 0.1 nm sites, behind a 0.17 F/m² inner layer), Butler–Volmer kinetics at the plane of closest approach (α = 0.125), the stagnant layer as on a rotating disk. The resistance is in series with the source, in Ω·m² of electrode: 0.1 Ω·m² is 10 kΩ on a 1 mm² drop.
