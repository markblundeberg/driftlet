A semiconductor electrode's Mott–Schottky plot

n-type silicon in KCl, the face between them blocking, its capacitance read from a simulated impedance measurement at each potential: 1/C² against the potential is a straight line while the silicon's surface is depleted of electrons, its slope giving the doping and its intercept the flat-band potential. The points against the exact theory of the same model, and a fit as an experiment would make one. Vary the doping, the salt, the frequency and the circuit the capacitance is read through, whether holes can gather at the surface, and the range the line is fitted over. About this simulation

A potential that pulls electrons away from the surface of n-type silicon leaves its donors bare, a layer of positive charge W deep that the solution's ions answer across the face. Its capacitance is ε/W, and in the depletion approximation W grows as the square root of the potential across it, so 1/C² = 2(E − Efb − kT/q)/(qεND): a line whose slope gives the doping and whose intercept, less kT/q, the flat-band potential (the kT/q is the tail of the electrons the depletion approximation leaves out). That's Mott and Schottky's analysis of a metal–semiconductor contact, applied to an electrode, as photo­electrochemists do to place a semiconductor's bands against a solution's redox levels. The line here is the exact Poisson–Boltzmann theory of the same model, worked out apart from the solver: electrons and holes in the silicon, K⁺ and Cl⁻ in the solution, each in Boltzmann equilibrium, the same displacement either side of the face, so the silicon's space-charge capacitance and the solution's own diffuse layer (Gouy and Chapman's) in series. The potential is against the standard hydrogen electrode; the flat band, about −0.19 V at 10¹⁶ cm⁻³, follows from the alignment the face is given (below). Read in series at a low frequency, the simulated capacitance follows the theory within 0.5% from 0.2 V below the flat band up (to 0.15 V short of inversion, where holes gather).

Where to fit. Close to the flat band the electrons' tail still bends the line: four kT/q above it, the exact 1/C² is still 4% above the straight line it approaches (at eight, 0.07%). Fitted from 0.1 V above the flat band, the doping comes out about 1% high and the flat band 2 to 4 mV low; from 0.3 V above (where the default fit starts, at 10¹⁶ cm⁻³), within 0.2% and 0.4 mV. Below the flat band the surface accumulates electrons and the capacitance rises steeply: the line turns flat toward the axis, and a fit across that bend is far off (drag the fit's start below the flat band).

Holes. Far enough positive, the bands bend by twice the Fermi level's distance from the intrinsic level, and holes, the silicon's minority carriers, would gather at the face (inversion). But something has to make them. There are no traps here to generate them, and the back contact's few take from minutes to months to arrive, so a sweep at seconds per point (the default) never sees them: the depletion layer goes on deepening (deep depletion), and the line goes on straight (the bands panel shows the holes' level apart from the electrons': they're out of equilibrium, too few for where the bands now sit). Given hours, or traps to make the holes as real silicon has, the surface does invert (choose holes in equilibrium: the steady state, its back contact holding holes too). The inversion layer then stops the depletion layer growing, and since the holes can't follow any frequency here (only below about 10⁻³ Hz), the measured 1/C² goes flat: the high-frequency curve of a MOS capacitor. The theory's line, every carrier following, bends instead. (Past about +0.8 V at 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ the inversion layer's holes pass the band's effective density of states, where Boltzmann statistics no longer describe real silicon; the library says so.) On the wide-gap oxides most Mott–Schottky work is done on (TiO₂, Fe₂O₃, ZnO), inversion never forms at all.

The solution's share. The same charge sits in the solution's diffuse layer, whose capacitance is in series with the silicon's. In 0.1 M salt it is over a thousand times the silicon's at 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ in depletion, and the line is the silicon's. Heavily doped silicon in dilute salt brings the two closer: at 10¹⁸ cm⁻³ in 1 mM, 8% of E − Efb drops in the solution at +0.3 V (the readout's share; of each small change of potential, 3.5%), and the fit gives the doping 2–3% high and the flat band a few mV low. (On a real electrode a Helmholtz layer adds a third capacitance in series, and surface states can pin the bands outright; neither is in this model.)

The circuit. An impedance analyser measures Z; a capacitance is a reading of it through an assumed circuit. A blocking electrode is nearly the solution's resistance R in series with C, and reading Z as R and C in series, C = −1/(ωZ″), gives C (a leaky electrode, with a reaction across C, would need its own circuit at low frequencies). Read as R and C in parallel, Cp = −Z″/(ω|Z|²), it gives C/(1 + (ωRC)²), and 1/Cp² = 1/C² + 2(ωR)² + (ωR)⁴C²: first an offset, which moves the fitted flat band (31 mV at 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ in 0.1 M at 1 MHz), then a slope that changes with the frequency and, at high enough frequencies, turns the line over. The series reading isn't immune either: the solution between the silicon and the reference is a dielectric too, a capacitance ε/L across its resistance, and where R is large (dilute salt) that shunt adds to Z″ well below the solution's own relaxation frequency, κ/(2πε): in 1 mM KCl, beside silicon at 10¹⁸ cm⁻³, the series reading has fallen by a third at 100 kHz. Here the solution is 100 µm (R = L/κ, from the ions' diffusion coefficients, 0.67 Ω·cm² in 0.1 M), with the silicon's own resistance in series, and both readings of the simulated Z follow that circuit's in depletion. A real cell's uncompensated resistance per area is often ten to a hundred times larger, and these effects then set in at lower frequencies. The plot also shows the points at a tenth and ten times the frequency: lines that don't agree are the standard warning. Frequency dispersion in measured Mott–Schottky plots has other causes too (surface states, a constant-phase double layer, a rough surface); these are only the reading and the cell.

What the model leaves out. It's a stand-in: real silicon in water grows an oxide, which makes it an electrolyte–insulator–semiconductor structure whose surface answers the pH (the flat band moving about 59 mV per pH unit on oxides), and holes at its surface oxidize it, so it isn't blocking past inversion; near and below the flat band it evolves hydrogen. The silicon here is ideal: Boltzmann statistics, every donor ionized, no traps or surface states. The face is blocking, with no Helmholtz layer and a fixed dipole for its alignment, 0.39 V (silicon's electron affinity against the solution, an assumption, not a datum). The solution is ideal KCl. Real analyses also stumble on the electrode's true area (1/C² goes as the area squared) and on ε. For the method's pitfalls in practice: Hankin et al., J. Mater. Chem. A 7, 26162 (2019), and Sivula, ACS Energy Lett. 6, 2549 (2021).
