A Daniell cell, discharged through a load

Zn | 0.1 M ZnSO₄ | separator | 0.1 M CuSO₄ | Cu, each solution 0.5 mm deep and still, the separator passing only sulfate. At open circuit each metal's Fermi level sits at its couple's redox level (Zn²⁺/Zn and Cu²⁺/Cu), and the cell voltage is the 1.10 V between them. Connect a load and current flows: each metal departs from its couple's level by an overpotential, the redox levels tilt across the electrolyte (ohmic drop, and the compositions changing near each electrode), and the voltage left for the load shrinks. Zinc dissolves, copper plates, and sulfate crosses the separator to keep each side neutral. The run goes on, in log time, until the cell is flat: 0.5 mm of 0.1 M Cu²⁺ holds 0.96 C/cm², or 1.06 J/cm² at 1.10 V. A light load drains it slowly, near the open-circuit voltage throughout, and gets 97% of that energy (the rest because the couples' levels close in as Cu²⁺ runs low and Zn²⁺ builds up). The heaviest load here asks for more current than diffusion can supply: the Cu²⁺ at the copper runs out early and the voltage sags, so although nearly all the charge still comes out, less than a fifth of the energy reaches the load. The rest is dissipated in the cell.

The first level diagram is electronic: the metals' Fermi levels, each couple's redox level, the electronic level at which the local mixture is in equilibrium, and their standard levels, which ride on the SHE's standard level (φ itself, with table μ°): zinc's 0.76 V below it, copper's 0.34 V above. The second is the same story ion by ion: each species' voltage Vi (thick) and standard level V°i (thin), with sulfate drawn 4.1 V lower (⌇) to share the plot. Zn²⁺'s voltage meets zinc's electrons, and Cu²⁺'s meets copper's: that's what equilibrium at each electrode means.
