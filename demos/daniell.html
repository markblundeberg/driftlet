A Daniell cell, its separator simulated ion by ion, discharged through a load

Zn | 0.1 M ZnSO₄ | 3 M KCl salt bridge | 0.1 M CuSO₄ | Cu, built in a tube: the two solutions are each 4 cm long and stirred, the bridge 2 cm of unstirred KCl, and everything per unit area of the tube's bore. (A real cell has beakers far bigger than its bridge's bore, so its solutions change more slowly; 1D can't hold that geometry.) At open circuit each metal's Fermi level sits at its couple's redox level, 1.10 V apart. Connect the load and current flows: each metal departs from its couple's level by an overpotential, the solutions and bridge drop some of the voltage, and the rest reaches the load. Zinc dissolves, copper plates, and the cell runs until the Cu²⁺ is gone.

The bridge is where a textbook says "assume the liquid junction potentials are negligible". Here it's simulated, ions and all. Every ion crosses each junction, so the bridge leaks: KCl diffuses out into the two solutions, and Zn²⁺, Cu²⁺ and SO₄²⁻ diffuse in, on the way to the far side. The zoomed diagram follows the zinc-side junction ion by ion: each species' voltage Vi changes by tens of mV across it, and those differences drive the leak, yet at open circuit φ steps by only about a millivolt (under load, φ also falls with the current's ohmic drop). That small step is the liquid junction potential: it starts at +0.97 mV, against +0.99 mV from Henderson's formula for these solutions, and the copper side's is −0.97 mV, nearly cancelling. That's why a KCl bridge works: K⁺ and Cl⁻ move at nearly the same speed. Over hours the leaked KCl also makes the solutions more conductive, so under a heavy load the voltage rises at first. Each end of the bridge starts as a thin zone where the solutions are pre-mixed, as at a frit (a perfectly sharp start is harder for the solver, and no real junction is sharp).

Daniell's own separator was an unglazed earthenware pot: a porous wall that passes every ion, only slowly (here 5 mm of pores, where ions diffuse ten times slower than in free solution). There's no KCl to leak, and the junction between the two sulfate solutions is tiny. Either way, though, copper sulfate seeps across, and Cu²⁺ that reaches the zinc plates straight onto it, a short circuit inside the cell that wastes its charge. The status line counts it (as the copper that's gone without passing through the load), and the slower the discharge, the bigger its share: at the lightest load here, over ten days, the bridged cell loses about a fifth of its charge this way and the potted one about a twentieth.
